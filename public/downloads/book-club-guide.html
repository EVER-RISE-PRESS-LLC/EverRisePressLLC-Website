Book club discussion sheet

How To Have a Financial Heart Attack by Lamont D. McLeod

Original EverRise Press prompts based on the full manuscript. Spoilers included. Readers may pass; nobody needs to disclose income, debts or account balances. Discuss the text and check the context of a passage in the book.

Ten questions

  1. Chapters 1–2: The allowance and the notebook. In “The Penny Candy Foundation,” McLeod looks forward to another Friday allowance. In “The Blueprint I Ignored,” he recalls his parents recording figures and keeping receipts. What does placing these memories side by side reveal? Where do you see observation without application?
  2. Chapter 3: What a new paycheck promises. In “The First Paycheck Syndrome,” he recalls receiving $98 on Friday after snack deductions and having $3 by Sunday night. What role does the expectation of next Friday play in the scene? Which details make the repeated pattern visible?
  3. Chapter 4: Planning the trip and the return. “Spring Break 1997: My First Real Financial Heart Attack” includes saved paychecks, money borrowed from his parents and costs beyond the trip itself. Which costs receive attention before he leaves, and which emerge later? How does the return journey change the meaning of the weekend?
  4. Chapters 5–8: Work, weekends and uncertainty. Across job changes and trips, how does the story connect an immediate decision with a later consequence? Distinguish the choices described in these chapters from the wider uncertainty that can surround employment. Which scene best shows that tension?
  5. Chapters 9–10: The cost of independence. McLeod chooses an Atlanta apartment by its low rent before seeing it. His move also brings family support, excitement and new bills. What does “affordable” seem to mean before the move, and what additional demands become visible afterward?
  6. Chapter 11: Knowing the system. In “College Life and Collections Calls,” he works in debt collection while struggling with his own spending. How does the contrast between professional knowledge and personal action shape the chapter? What can information explain, and what does the story suggest information alone has not changed?
  7. Chapter 12: Shared bills and private space. In “The Roommate Lesson,” the roommate pays his agreed half. The conflict involves the trade-off between lower costs and having the home to himself. How does that detail affect your reading? What gets lost if we assume the roommate failed to pay?
  8. Chapter 13: Listening to Craig. “CompuCredit and the Man Who Changed Everything” brings a direct conversation about his choices. How does Craig’s approach differ from advice heard earlier in the book? What makes this exchange stand out without assuming that one conversation resolves the whole pattern?
  9. Chapter 14: Choosing one action. “The Awakening Begins” places advice beside the pull of familiar payday habits. What is the difference between agreeing with a point and practicing it in a later scene? Choose one moment where that distance becomes clear.
  10. Chapter 15: An unfinished reckoning. “The Reckoning” points toward a future second book rather than presenting an instant, completed recovery. How does that open ending affect your response? What remains unresolved in this volume, and what would you want a later account to examine?

A flexible 60-minute meeting

0–10 minutes: opening impressions. 10–30: Chapters 1–8. 30–50: Chapters 9–15. 50–60: a closing question. Choose a few prompts rather than rushing through all ten.

Closing question: Which detail will you read differently after this conversation?
